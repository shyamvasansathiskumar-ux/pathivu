1

The idea

A notebook with a stopwatch.

When a company's AI does something harmful, the law can give the company a few hours to report it. Pathivu writes down what happened, works out the deadline, and makes sure nobody can change the record afterwards.

Companies now use AI for real work: writing code, answering customers, sorting hospital samples. Sometimes it goes wrong. An AI assistant deletes data it shouldn't. A chatbot shows one customer another customer's phone number. A scoring system treats people from villages worse than people from cities.

In India, some of these mistakes come with a legal duty to report, and a deadline. Pathivu (பதிவு, Tamil for record) is the tool a company would use in those first hours: to write it all down properly, to see which deadlines apply, and to prove later exactly who did what and when.

2

A story

Monday afternoon at a ride app.

A ride-booking company uses an AI coding assistant. It was asked to tidy up a test database. It had more access than it needed, and it deleted the real bookings database instead. Nobody attacked anyone. It was simply a mistake. Here is how the afternoon looks to Pathivu:

It happened1:40 pm

The assistant runs the wrong command. Bookings disappear.

Someone noticed1:55 pm

An engineer sees errors on the dashboard.

The company knew2:00 pm

The manager is told. This is the moment the clock starts.

Report due8:00 pm

India's cyber security agency, CERT-In, expects a report within 6 hours.

Pathivu keeps those three times separate on purpose. "When it happened", "when someone noticed" and "when the company knew" are often different, and the law counts from the last one. Once saved, none of the three can ever be edited, because moving "when the company knew" by even an hour would move the deadline too.

Report to CERT-In by 8:00 pm, same day.
3

Eight words

The only words you need.

Incident

Something that went wrong with an AI system. Like an accident report after a car crash.

Harm

Who or what got hurt by it: lost data, leaked phone numbers, people treated unfairly, money lost.

CERT-In India

The government team that handles cyber security incidents. Its rule since 2022: report within 6 hours of noticing.

DPDP India

India's data protection law. From 13 May 2027 its Rule 7 says: if personal data leaks, tell the people affected and the Data Protection Board without delay.

Framework

A shared list of the ways AI fails, made by experts (OWASP, MITRE ATLAS, NIST). Like the codes a doctor uses to name an illness, so everyone means the same thing.

Audit trail

A diary of every action: who did what, and when. In Pathivu it can only be added to, never edited.

Hash

A short fingerprint of some text, worked out by maths. Change one letter and the fingerprint changes completely. You'll try one in part 5.

Database

Where the records are kept, here MySQL. Think of a vault with its own guards. The guards (called triggers) check every change before letting it in.

4

Why not a spreadsheet

The problem with a pencil.

A company could keep all this in a spreadsheet. But in a spreadsheet anyone can quietly change the time a problem was found, delete an awkward row, or type "report sent at 7:55 pm" when it actually went at 9:30. Months later, when a regulator asks questions, nobody can tell what was changed.

Pathivu works like an old bank ledger written in ink. You never rub anything out. A correction is a new line that says what changed and why.

The important part: these rules are not only inside the app. They are built into the database itself. So even someone who skips the app and types commands into the database directly gets the same answer: no.

5

Five locks

How the record protects itself.

i

Times are written in ink

The three times in the story can't be edited. If one was wrong, a correction is added beside it with a reason, and the deadline stays where it was.

ii

The database keeps its own clock

When a report is marked "sent", the database writes the time itself, from its own clock. The app can't type in a nicer time. It can't even trick the database by setting its clock back: Pathivu checks for that and refuses.

iii

Two people for every legal decision

Like a cheque that needs two signatures. The person who writes a report can't approve it. The person who reported an incident can't close it or decide a deadline doesn't apply. Four eyes, every time.

iv

Every entry carries the fingerprint of the one before

The diary is a chain. Each entry's fingerprint is made from its own words and the previous entry's fingerprint. Change any old entry and every fingerprint after it stops matching. Try it below.

v

A copy of the last page, kept somewhere else

Someone with full control of the computer could rewrite an entry and then redo every fingerprint after it. So Pathivu lets you save the newest fingerprint outside the database (email it to yourself, print it). That saved copy is called an anchor, and a rewritten diary won't match it.

6

What it refuses

Things people try, and the answer.

The project comes with 110 automatic tests. 65 of them are attacks: they talk to the database directly, the way a bug or a hacker would, and try to break a rule. Every one has to be refused. Some of them, in plain words:

Change when the company found out

refused

That time starts every deadline, so it is written once.

Set the database's clock back, then mark the report "sent"

refused

The database notices its clock was changed for this session.

Delete an incident

refused

The app's database account has no permission to delete anything, anywhere.

Approve your own report

refused

Two different people, always.

Close an incident you reported yourself

refused

Someone else must check it first.

Quietly reword what happened

refused

A change to the description needs a written reason, kept forever.

Say a missed deadline "didn't apply", afterwards

refused

Once the time has passed, it stays missed, or shows as reported late.

Edit an old diary entry

refused

The diary only grows. If someone with full control forces a change, the fingerprints show it.

8

Questions

Things people usually ask.

Is this a real product a company uses?

Not yet. It is a working student project with pretend data. It runs, it has real tests, and the legal deadlines it uses are real, but no company depends on it.

Does it send reports to the government?

No. A person submits the report the official way, then records in Pathivu that it was sent. The database stamps the time.

Does it use AI to decide things?

No. It suggests categories using simple, readable rules, and a person must confirm each one. It also never decides whether the law was broken. It starts clocks cautiously ("unsure" counts as "yes"), and a named reviewer decides.

Why would anyone change a record on purpose?

Because a missed deadline can mean fines and bad news stories. The easiest cover-up is to change one time or one word after the fact. Pathivu is designed so that this is either impossible or visible.

Can someone with full control of the computer still cheat?

They can switch the guards off. That is the honest limit of any database. But the fingerprint chain shows exactly which entry changed, and a saved anchor catches even a complete rewrite. Both are tested.

Why the Tamil name?

பதிவு (pathivu) means "record" or "registration" in Tamil. The project was built in Chennai, and keeping a record is its whole job.
